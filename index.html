General Biology — Unit Reference

The cell is the smallest unit that still counts as alive.

This reference walks through what is inside a cell, how prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells differ, how cells are classified, and how a general cell changes shape and structure to specialize for a single job.

Part 01 — Organelles

Structure and function of major subcellular organelles

Covers both eukaryotic and prokaryotic organelles.

Eukaryotic cells

StructureDescriptionFunction
Cell membraneThin and flexible layer around the cell.Controls what enters and leaves the cell.
CytoplasmJelly-like substance inside the cell.Holds organelles and is where many chemical reactions occur.
NucleusMembrane-bound structure that contains DNA.Controls cell activities and stores genetic information.
NucleolusThe dense region inside the nucleus.Responsible for the production of ribosomal components.
RibosomesSmall structures composed of RNA and proteins.Responsible for protein production.
MitochondriaBean-shaped organelles with folded inner membranes.Produces energy in the form of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) for the cell.
Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)A network of membranes.Makes and transports proteins and lipids.
Golgi apparatusStack of flattened membrane sacs.Modifies, sorts and packages proteins.
LysosomesSmall membrane-bound sacs containing enzymes.Breaks down waste and damaged cell parts.
VacuolesMembrane-bound storage sacs.Stores water, nutrients and waste.
CytoskeletonNetwork of protein fibers.Maintains cell shape and helps movement.
ChloroplastAn organelle with a distinctly green pigment containing chlorophyll.Performs photosynthesis.
Cell wallRigid outer layer outside the membrane.Supports and protects the cell.

Prokaryotic cells

StructureDescriptionFunction
Plasma membraneThin phospholipid bilayer containing proteins.Controls what enters and leaves the cell; involved in respiration, energy production, and cell signaling.
Cell wallRigid layer outside the plasma membrane. In bacteria, mainly made of peptidoglycan.Maintains cell shape and protects the cell from bursting due to osmotic pressure.
Capsule / slime layerSticky outer layer made mainly of polysaccharides or proteins.Protects against drying and immune defenses; helps cells attach to surfaces and form biofilms.
CytoplasmJelly-like interior containing water, enzymes, ions, nutrients, and cellular components.Site of many metabolic reactions.
NucleoidRegion containing the cell's main circular DNA chromosome; not surrounded by a membrane.Stores genetic information and controls cellular activities.
PlasmidsSmall, usually circular pieces of DNA separate from the main chromosome.Carry additional genes, such as antibiotic resistance genes; can be transferred between cells.
RibosomesSmall complexes of rRNA and proteins; prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S.Manufacture proteins by translating mRNA.
FlagellumLong, helical protein filament attached to a basal body and motor.Provides movement or locomotion.
Pili / fimbriaeShort protein filaments extending from the cell surface.Help cells attach to surfaces; some pili enable DNA transfer between bacteria.
Inclusion bodiesStorage granules or particles within the cytoplasm.Store nutrients and energy reserves such as glycogen, lipids, or phosphate.
Part 02 — Comparison

Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, by distinguishing feature

Twelve features compared side by side.

FeatureProkaryotic cellsEukaryotic cells
DefinitionSingle-cell organisms, no nucleus.Complex organisms, with nucleus.
ExamplesBacteria, archaea.Animals, plants, fungi.
Cell sizeSmaller, typically 0.1–5.0 µm in diameter.Larger, usually 10–100 µm in diameter.
MitochondriaAbsent; energy produced in the cytoplasm.Present; powerhouse of the cell.
CytoplasmPresent; site of metabolic processes.Present; holds organelles in place.
Endoplasmic reticulumAbsent; simpler protein synthesis.Present; crucial for protein synthesis.
PlasmidsPresent; small, circular DNA.Rare, except in some fungi and plants.
NucleusAbsent; DNA floats freely.Present; contains and protects.
DNA organellesFew; simpler structures.Many specialized functions.
DNA structureSingle, circular chromosome.Multiple, linear chromosomes.
Ribosomes70S; smaller.80S; larger.
LocomotionSimple flagella; direct movement.Complex flagella or cilia for movement.

Cell types and the function of each structure

Prokaryotic, then eukaryotic animal and plant cells.

Prokaryotic cells

StructureFunction
Nucleoid regionHolds free-floating genetic material (DNA) without a nucleus.
RibosomesSynthesize proteins for the cell.
Cell wallProvides rigid structural support and protection.
FlagellaEnables movement through liquid environments.

Eukaryotic animal cells

StructureFunction
NucleusHouses DNA and controls cell activities.
MitochondriaGenerates energy (ATP) through cellular respiration.
Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)Synthesizes proteins (rough ER) and lipids (smooth ER).
LysosomesDigest food and break down unwanted materials.
CentriolesHelp in cell division.
FlagellaAllows the cell to move.

Eukaryotic plant cells

StructureFunction
Central vacuoleStores water, food, and waste while maintaining turgor pressure.
Cell wallOuter covering containing cellulose that provides rigidity.
PlastidsResponsible for photosynthesis and pigment storage, for example chloroplasts.
PlasmodesmataConnect adjacent plant cells for transport and communication.
Part 04 — Specialized cells

Specialized cells and their primary function

Animal, stomach, and plant cell types.

Specialized animal cells

Cell typePrimary function
Red blood cells (erythrocytes)Carry and deliver oxygen throughout the body using hemoglobin.
White blood cells (leukocytes)Protect the body against infections and foreign pathogens.
Nerve cells (neurons)Transmit electrical and chemical signals throughout the body.
Muscle cells (myocytes)Contract to enable movement and exert force.
Liver cells (hepatocytes)Produce and process materials, detoxify chemicals, and synthesize proteins.
MicrovilliIncrease surface area of intestinal and epithelial cells for nutrient absorption.

Specialized stomach cells

Cell typeFunction
Chief cellsDigest proteins by secreting pepsinogen using hemoglobin.
Parietal cellsProduce hydrochloric acid (HCl) and intrinsic factor for nutrient absorption.
Mucous cellsProvide a protective mucus covering against stomach acid.

Specialized plant cells

Cell typePrimary function
Parenchyma cellsPerform photosynthesis, store nutrients, and aid in tissue repair.
Collenchyma cellsProvide flexible support to stems and leaves.
Sclerenchyma cellsProvide rigid structural support and strength.
Xylem cellsTransport water and dissolved minerals upward from roots.
Phloem cellsTransport sugars and nutrients throughout the plant.
Part 05 — Specialization

Cell modifications that enable specialization

How structure changes to fit function.

Cell modifications are the changes in a cell's structure, shape, size, and cellular components that let it perform a specific function efficiently. As cells specialize, they develop unique features suited to their particular roles, which allows different cell types to work together and maintain the normal processes of an organism.

Cell typeModificationHow it helps the cell function
Red blood cells (erythrocytes)Lose their nucleus and develop a biconcave shape.Allows them to carry and transport oxygen more efficiently.
Nerve cells (neurons)Develop long extensions called axons and dendrites.Enables the transmission of electrical signals throughout the body.
Muscle cellsContain numerous mitochondria and specialized contractile proteins.Provide energy and allow the cells to contract and produce movement.
Hair cellsDevelop long, thin extensions that increase surface area.Enhances the absorption of water and minerals from the soil.
